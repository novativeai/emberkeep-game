# Opening-scene research (source notes for opening-scene.md)

Study of how games open — specifically how a **narrator-led opening** earns the
first minute — done 2026-08-04 to ground [opening-scene.md](../opening-scene.md).
Three bodies of evidence: mobile first-session retention data, the merge genre's
own openings, and craft writing on exposition. Numbers are "accurate as of the
cited period"; the craft conclusions are stable, the benchmarks drift yearly.

## A. The attention budget is small and measurable

- **D1 retention benchmarks sit around 27%**, D7 8–14%, D30 3–7%; puzzle/match
  leads, hyper-casual drops fastest. That is the pool the opening is spending.
- **The tutorial is one of the largest single drop-off points.** The recurring
  recommendation is to keep it **under five minutes** and to make it skippable
  or fast-forwardable. The named causes of first-session churn are a tutorial
  that runs long, a first session with no clear goal, and anything that stalls
  progress before the player has a reason to trust the app.
- Retention is **not** predictable from a single session's data — it takes a
  day, really a week, before behaviour predicts churn. Practical reading: the
  opening cannot be A/B-tuned into working. It has to be written well the first
  time, on craft grounds, because the metric will not tell you in time.

**Conclusion.** Every tap-gated line before the player's first action is spend,
not investment. Budget the opening in *seconds to first touch*, and make the
lines carry more than one job each.

## B. How the genre leader actually opens

Merge Mansion — the genre's narrative benchmark — opens with **a person being
vague, not with lore**:

- Maddie's wedding is called off; her grandmother Ursula consoles her by handing
  over a family mansion **that has never once been mentioned to her**, plus a set
  of keys, plus a deadline (renovate or it is condemned).
- Ursula is **"as vague as possible,"** which leaves Maddie — and the player —
  with more questions than they started with. The mystery is *manufactured in
  dialogue*, from withholding, not from worldbuilding.
- The company's marketing went **all-in on that meta layer** rather than on the
  merge mechanic. The hook that sells the game is "What's Grandma hiding?"

**Conclusion.** The genre's proven opening is not "here is the history of this
place." It is "here is a person who will not tell you something." That costs no
systems and no player memory — a withheld answer is remembered because it itches.

## C. What survives a first session

- A useful working rule from narrative-design practice: **"If the player only
  heard it, and didn't see it or interact with it, presume they won't remember
  it."** It pushes exposition toward the tangible.
- The medium's baseline assumption is that the audience **can and will skip** —
  players ignore conversations and skip cutscenes. Anything load-bearing must
  therefore also be true of the board, the art, or the first action.
- The counter-practice to dumping is to let players **find** the story through
  NPCs, interface and optional depth, rather than shovelling it at them.

**Conclusion.** Facts stated in the opening are the *least* durable thing in it.
Write the opening to deliver a **question, a relationship and a verb** — those
survive. Save the facts for the moment the player can act on them.

## D. The withholding mentor

Both of the reference points for Emberkeep's guide converge on the same build:

- **Hecate (Hades II)** — mentor and guide; "discipline, ritual, patience";
  strict and rigorous but a source of comfort. Supergiant's stated philosophy is
  **narrative economy**: trust the player to understand the world through action
  rather than exposition. When she beats you she *teaches* — comments on your
  technique, your haste, your lack of focus. Her big secret is **about herself**:
  she is a Melinoë from another timeline who won and was sanded down by it.
- **Ursula (Merge Mansion)** — reveals family mysteries slowly as areas unlock;
  the withholding is the retention spine, not a single twist.

**Conclusion, and it is the important one.** The mentor is trustworthy about the
*world* and evasive about *herself*. That split is what makes a withholding
tutor tolerable rather than annoying: the player never doubts the instructions,
so they never resent the evasion. It also means the secret can sit unresolved
for a whole campaign without the guide becoming useless.

This is already the authoring rule recorded on EMB-25 — the research confirms it
rather than discovering it.

## E. Rules derived for Emberkeep

1. **The world before the framing.** The player should see the ash before anyone
   explains it. A held, silent beat costs nothing and buys the only genuinely
   uninterpreted impression the game will ever get.
2. **Open three questions, answer one.** *What happened here · who is she · why
   me.* Answer only "why me", and answer it practically enough that the player
   notices it is not the whole truth.
3. **The withholding must be audible.** A secret the player cannot tell is being
   kept is not a hook, it is an absence. She has to stop herself mid-sentence
   where the player can hear the door close.
4. **Proper-noun budget: one.** Every unexplained capitalised noun is a tax on a
   player who has been in the game for nine seconds. Spend it on *Emberkeep* —
   it is on the icon. "Daughters of the Moon", "the Great Flame" and "Borealis"
   are all cheaper later, when something pays them off.
5. **Start on the player, not on the world.** "Long ago, this place blazed…" has
   no stake for someone with no stake yet. Start with her looking at you.
6. **She is oblique about herself, plain about the rules.** The voice encodes the
   authoring rule, so lore lines and teaching lines sound different and the
   player learns the difference by ear.
7. **The narrator speaks the player's questions.** The Keeper never talks. A
   monologue becomes a conversation when she answers things you have not asked
   and says so — "You'll want to know…", "Don't ask me that yet."
8. **End on a verb.** The last line before the tutorial hands over a physical
   action, not a sentiment.
9. **Survive the skip.** Cut the scene entirely and the player must still know:
   the place is ruined, a woman wants it fixed, they are the one who can. If the
   script fails that test it is carrying too much.
